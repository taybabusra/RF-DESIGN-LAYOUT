
## Question1
The same signal can be viewed in the frequency domain. What does that view reveal that the time domain does not?

<table>
<tr>
<td width="50%">

### Myself
The time domain shows how a signal evolves, the frequency domain shows what it is made of, and how systems will shape it.

</td>
<td width="50%">

### Answer
The frequency-domain view shows how the signal's energy is distributed across frequency, revealing which tones or bands are present and how strong each is. That makes it the natural view for questions about pitch, harmonics, noise spread, and which filter would help. Information obvious here, such as a faint tone buried in noise, can be invisible in the time-domain waveform.

</td>
</tr>

## Question2
Experienced RF designers treat PCB layout as part of the circuit, not an afterthought. What does that mean in practice?

<table>
<tr>
<td width="50%">

### Myself
At RF frequencies, the physical geometry creates and modifies the electrical function, so design correctness depends as much on spatial structure as on schematic design.

</td>
<td width="50%">

### Answer
It means the layout itself realizes transmission lines, matching elements, grounding, and shielding, so trace dimensions, component placement, via patterns, and plane cuts are design parameters chosen deliberately. Parasitics from the layout are anticipated and either used or controlled. In practice, you design the schematic and layout together and simulate with layout parasitics included, because at RF the board is part of the circuit.

</td>
</tr>
</table>

## Question3
Glitch power can be a surprisingly real fraction of total dynamic power. Where do the glitches that cause it come from?

<table>
<tr>
<td width="50%">

### Myself
Glitch power is generated by unintended intermediate switching caused by unequal propagation delays inside combinational logic networks.

</td>
<td width="50%">

### Answer
Glitches are spurious transitions that occur when inputs to a gate arrive at different times because of unbalanced path delays, so the output briefly switches to a wrong value before settling. Each of those extra transitions charges and discharges capacitance, and that dissipates energy just like a real transition. Deep, unbalanced combinational logic produces the most glitching, so it can be a meaningful hidden power cost.

</td>
</tr>
</table>

## Question4
What does the term return loss mean, and what range is generally considered acceptable for a well-matched RF port at 2.4 GHz?

<table>
<tr>
<td width="50%">

### Myself
Return loss measures how cleanly RF energy enters a system without bouncing back, and for 2.4 GHz ports, ~15–20 dB is the practical well-matched sweet spot, with ≥20 dB considered high-quality RF matching.

</td>
<td width="50%">

### Answer
Return loss is a measure of how much power is reflected back from an RF port compared to the power that is transmitted into the port. It is defined using the reflection coefficient, and it indicates impedance matching quality. A higher return loss means better matching and less reflected energy. For a well-matched RF port at 2.4 GHz, 15–20 dB is generally acceptable, while 20 dB or higher indicates excellent matching with minimal reflection and efficient power transfer.
</td>
</tr>
</table>

## Question5
As an RF signal's frequency rises, its wavelength shrinks. Why does that relationship change how you treat a physical trace?

<table>
<tr>
<td width="50%">

### Myself
When frequency rises, wavelength shrinks, and the trace becomes electrically “long” relative to the signal.
So it stops behaving like a simple wire (lumped circuit) and behaves like a transmission line.
That forces you to treat it in terms of i) Controlled impedance, ii) Reflections (mismatch effects), iii)Delay/phase along the trace, 
high frequency turns routing from “connect points” into “manage wave behavior.”

</td>
<td width="50%">

### Answer
As the frequency of an RF signal increases, its wavelength decreases, which significantly impacts how we treat physical traces in RF design. When the wavelength becomes comparable to the length of the trace, the trace no longer behaves like a simple lumped element but rather as a transmission line. This transition necessitates a different approach to design and analysis. We must consider controlled impedance to ensure that the signal is transmitted efficiently without reflections. Mismatches in impedance can lead to reflections that degrade signal quality, so careful design is essential. Additionally, the delay and phase shifts along the trace become critical factors to manage, as they can affect the timing and integrity of the signal. In essence, at high frequencies, routing is not just about connecting points; it involves managing wave behavior to maintain signal fidelity and performance in the RF system.
<br>
Wavelength is the speed of propagation divided by frequency, so higher frequency means a shorter wavelength. Once a trace length is a meaningful fraction of a wavelength, the signal's phase changes appreciably along it, and the trace behaves as a transmission line rather than a simple wire. So the same physical length that is negligible at low frequency demands controlled impedance and matching at RF.
</td>
</tr>
</table>
